Transformers, Inside Out

Most tutorials build a transformer from the bottom up. You meet tokenizers, embeddings and attention formulas long before you see the model do anything. This course goes the other way. We take a real language model, ask it one question, and then open it up one layer at a time until we can rebuild it from scratch. Part 2 then makes it practical: we make it fast, shrink it, open the file format local apps run, and teach it something new.

Running example 1 · How does it know?

This is the sentence we follow through every lesson. Nothing in the prompt says "Paris", so the answer has to come from something the model learned. The model is SmolLM2-135M, a small open model with the same design as Llama. It reads these ten tokens and gives ' Paris' an 88% chance of coming next. By the end you will know where each part of that answer comes from.

How to use this page

Each lesson starts with a question, explains the idea in plain words, and then gives you an instrument to play with. Every lesson has a matching script in lessons/ that produces the same numbers on your own machine (get them on GitHub; a laptop CPU is enough).

Two kinds of box follow the running examples: amber Our sentence boxes for Paris, teal Copying thread boxes for Robby.

Try to predict what will happen before you move a slider. Being wrong is where the learning happens.

How much math?

A handful of short formulas, each introduced only when you need it. Part 1 has four: softmax, temperature, loss and attention, plus three one-line helpers (RMSNorm and SwiGLU in lesson 05, RoPE in lesson 07). Part 1 · How it thinks

Lesson 01 · lessons/01_black_box.py

Use it as a black box first

Before we open anything: what does this thing actually do?

You give it text, and it continues the text. That is the whole interface. There is no question-answering module, no database of facts, no grammar engine. Everything you see below comes from one trick, repeated: guess the next piece of text.

These pieces are called tokens. A token can be a whole word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark, and this model knows 49,152 of them. For now, think "roughly a word"; lesson 04 shows exactly how text is cut up.

The model we use is a base model. It has read a huge amount of web text, code and books, but nobody has taught it to chat. It does not "answer" you; it writes whatever would most plausibly come next in a document. That is why the prompts below look like the start of a sentence instead of a question.

Five prompts, greedy decoding

always picks the single most likely next token

Prompt in normal text, the model's continuation in colour. Run it again and you get exactly the same output, because greedy decoding involves no randomness.

Look at what it is good and bad at. Code and counting are almost perfect, because they follow strict patterns. Stories are fluent. Facts are hit and miss. Our running example comes out right, but "The capital of France is" falls into a loop: "the capital of the country. The capital of France is the capital of…". A 135-million-parameter model (parameters are the learned numbers inside the model; lesson 04 shows what they look like) is small (the models behind chat assistants are hundreds or thousands of times larger), and its weak spots are easy to find. That makes it a good specimen: you can see the gears.

Greedy decoding loops because once a phrase becomes likely, repeating it makes it even more likely. The cure is to let chance in:

Same prompt, three runs with sampling

temperature 0.8, top-k 50

With sampling, the model rolls a weighted die for each token. Two of the three runs start with " named", the most likely next token, and one of them even lands on "Robby" again. The other run picked a less likely first token, and the story went somewhere else entirely. One early roll of the die changes everything after it.

Our sentence

"The Eiffel Tower is located in the city of" → " Paris, France. It is the tallest building in the world." The first word is right, and then the model confidently invents a fact. Remember this: the model's job is to produce plausible text, and true text is only usually the most plausible.

Key ideas

  • A language model continues text. Chat behaviour is extra training layered on top.
  • Greedy decoding is repeatable but loops. Sampling is lively but less reliable.
  • Small models are great for learning because their failures are easy to see.

Lesson 02 · lessons/02_next_token.py

What comes out: a probability for every token

If the model doesn't output a paragraph, what does it output?

The single most important fact about these models:

A language model is a function. Text goes in. A score for every possible next token comes out.

The model has a fixed vocabulary of 49,152 tokens (words and pieces of words, see lesson 04). One call gives back 49,152 numbers called logits, one per token. A higher logit means "more likely to come next".

Logits are raw scores and can be any size. To turn them into probabilities that add up to 1 we use softmax, which is the first of our five formulas:

p(token) = exp(logit) / sum of exp(all logits)

The exp makes everything positive and stretches the gaps: a logit that is 4 points higher becomes about 55 times more likely. For our sentence, ' Paris' scores 10.6 and the runner-up ' T' scores 6.5. A 4-point lead turns into 88% against 1.5%.

The output of one forward pass

real logits, softmax computed in your browser

The top 15 of 49,152 tokens. The grey line under the bars shows how much probability is spread over the remaining 49,137 tokens. A leading space is drawn as · because it is part of the token: ' Paris' and 'Paris' are different tokens.

Temperature: dividing before softmax

Move the temperature slider above. Temperature is the second formula, and it's just a division:

p = softmax(logits / T)

Dividing by a small T (below 1) makes the gaps between logits bigger, so the winner takes almost everything. Dividing by a large T shrinks the gaps, so the distribution flattens and unlikely tokens get a real chance. At T close to 0 you get greedy decoding. Notice how differently the prompts behave: "Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday," stays confident until the temperature gets very high, while "My favourite food is" is spread thin even at T = 1, because there are many good answers.

It predicts at every position at once

Here's something tutorials rarely show. The model doesn't only produce a guess for the end of the text. It produces a guess after every token, all in the same pass. Generation only uses the last one, but the others show you what the model expected along the way:

The model's guess after each token of our sentence

hover or tap a token

After ·E it has no idea (it guesses "-"). After iffel it predicts ·Tower at 87%. After ·in it already bets on ·Paris at 57%, before it has even seen "the city of".

This is also why training is efficient: one pass over a 1,000-token document gives 1,000 predictions to check, not one.

Loss: how surprised was the model?

When you train a model you need one number that says how wrong it was. That number is the loss, and it is formula three:

loss = −log( probability the model gave to the real next token )

If the model gave the real token 100%, the loss is 0. At 50% the loss is 0.69, at 1% it is 4.6, and it keeps growing the more surprised the model is. Training is nothing more than nudging the weights so this number goes down, averaged over trillions of tokens. We never train in this course, but loss is a superb measuring stick, and we'll use it to test the model in lessons 07 and 09.

Per-token surprise: Paris vs. Berlin

loss of each token given everything before it

The two sentences share the same first ten tokens, so their losses match until the city. ' Paris' costs 0.13 (88% expected) and ' Berlin' costs 7.96 (0.03% expected). Look at ' E' too: at the start of a sentence, almost nothing predicts "E", so it costs 7.9. Then 'iffel' costs 3.2 and ' Tower' only 0.14, because once you have "Eiffel", "Tower" is obvious.

Key ideas

  • One forward pass gives one logit per vocabulary token, at every position.
  • Softmax turns logits into probabilities; temperature divides the logits first.
  • Loss is −log(p of the real token). Low loss means the model saw it coming.

Lesson 03 · lessons/03_generate_loop.py

Writing a paragraph is a for-loop

How does one-token-at-a-time turn into a story?

model.generate() looks like magic, but it's a loop that you can write in ten lines:

lessons/03_generate_loop.py

def my_generate_greedy(prompt, n_new=20):
    ids = encode(tok, prompt, device)
    for _ in range(n_new):
        logits = model(ids).logits          # lesson 02: scores for every token
        next_id = logits[0, -1].argmax()    # take the best one at the LAST position
        ids = torch.cat([ids, next_id.view(1, 1)], dim=1)   # append it
    return ids                              # ...and go round again

That's it. Run it next to the library's model.generate(do_sample=False) and you get identical token IDs. The model has no memory between steps and no plan for the paragraph. Each step it re-reads everything written so far, including its own previous outputs, and scores the next token. Step through it:

Step through generation

greedy: the top bar always wins

The 10 best candidates for the next token

Watch the right-hand number as you step. After "a little robot", 6 tokens cover 90% of the probability, because ·named is a strong pattern. Right after "named", it takes 865 tokens to cover 90%: any name would do. The model's uncertainty rises and falls with every word, and that's exactly where sampling settings matter.

Copying thread

Keep stepping until the name "Robby" comes back (step 11). The first time, "by" after "Rob" was one option among 87. The second time, a single token covers 90%. Nothing about robots or cities makes "Robby" the right name. The model is certain because the name is already in the text, so it copies it. Hold that thought. Lesson 07 finds the heads that do this copying, and lesson 08 switches them off.

Sampling: three knobs that edit the probabilities

Greedy decoding always takes the top bar. Sampling rolls a weighted die instead. Real systems first edit the probability list with three knobs:

  • temperature: divide the logits by T (lesson 02).
  • top-k: keep only the k best tokens and throw the rest away.
  • top-p (also called nucleus sampling): keep the smallest set of best tokens whose probabilities add up to p. When the model is confident that might be 2 tokens; when it is unsure it might be 800.

The surviving probabilities are rescaled to add up to 1 and the die is rolled. Try it:

Sampling lab

struck-through tokens can no longer be picked

Probabilities here are exact over the full vocabulary for temperature, and use the top 50 tokens for the rolls. Set top-k to 1 and you have greedy decoding again. Set temperature to 3 with top-p at 100% and watch the junk tokens start winning; that is the "word salad" you saw at T = 1.8 in the lesson script.

Our sentence

For our prompt the model is so sure (' Paris' 88%) that temperature 0.7 with top-p 0.9 leaves only ' Paris' in the running. The knobs only make a difference where the model is unsure, which for our sentence happens after "Paris", when it has to decide what to say next.

Key ideas

  • Generation = predict, pick, append, repeat. The model writes nothing by itself; your loop does.
  • Temperature, top-k and top-p are edits to one probability list before a die roll.
  • The model's uncertainty changes at every step. "Tokens needed for 90%" makes that visible.

Lesson 04 · lessons/04_tokenizer.py

What goes in: tokens, then vectors

The model takes integers. Where do they come from, and what does it do with them?

Text goes through two steps before the neural network sees anything:

"Hello world" → tokenizer → [19556, 905] → embedding table → 2 vectors of 576 numbers

The tokenizer is not part of the neural network. It's a fixed rulebook, built once from text statistics with an algorithm called byte-pair encoding (BPE). BPE starts from single bytes and repeatedly glues together the most frequent neighbouring pair, 48,900 times. Frequent words end up as one token, rare words are spelled out from pieces, and any text at all can be written as bytes, so nothing is ever "unknown".

The playground below runs the real tokenizer in your browser, with the same vocabulary and the same 48,900 merge rules as the Python library. It has been checked to give identical IDs. Type anything.

Tokenizer playground

live, in your browser

Things to try, and what you'll find:

  • Spaces belong to the next word. "Hello world" is Hello ·world. So ' Paris' and 'Paris' are different tokens with different IDs.
  • Case matters. "Hello" and "hello" are unrelated IDs. The model has to learn that they mean the same thing.
  • Digits are always split one by one in this tokenizer, so "1234567" is seven tokens. That's a deliberate choice that helps with arithmetic.
  • Languages are not treated equally. The English sentence is 6 tokens; the same sentence in Hindi is 28. Scripts the tokenizer saw rarely get spelled out byte by byte. That means higher cost, a shorter effective context, and usually worse quality. Switch on "raw byte-level form" to see the bytes.

Our sentence

Ten tokens: . "Eiffel" is not in the vocabulary, so it arrives as ·E + iffel. The model never sees the word "Eiffel". It sees two fragments and has to work out from context that together they mean the famous tower. Keep an eye on iffel: in lesson 07 we'll find the head that carries it forward to the word that has to answer.

From ID to vector: the embedding table

Now the network starts. Its first layer is a table with one row per token: 49,152 rows of 576 numbers each. Looking up token 7042 means "take row 7042". There's no calculation, just a lookup. Here are the first 24 of the 576 numbers for ·Paris:

Each cell is one number (teal positive, amber negative). On its own a number means nothing; meaning lives in the pattern across all 576.

Nobody chose these numbers. They started random and were adjusted during training. Because tokens used in similar ways get adjusted in similar ways, similar tokens end up with similar rows. We can measure "similar" with cosine similarity, which looks only at the direction a vector points in (1 = same direction, 0 = unrelated).

Nearest neighbours

The embedding space, flattened

576 dims → 2 with PCA

The map on the right squashes 576 dimensions down to 2, so it loses a lot, but the groups are still there: days with days, numbers with numbers, cities near countries. ·king's closest neighbours include ' queen', ' emperor' and ' prince'. ·Paris sits next to ' London', ' Berlin' and ' France'. The model hasn't done any thinking yet, and the starting vectors already contain a rough map of meaning.

What a layer is, physically

Before we go inside, one word we'll use constantly: weights. The embedding table is a grid of learned numbers, 49,152 rows by 576 columns. Almost every other layer in the model is also a grid of learned numbers (a matrix). The only difference is how it's used. The embedding table looks up one row. A normal layer mixes instead: each of its output numbers is a weighted sum of all the input numbers, and the grid holds those weights.

576 numbers in → multiply by a 576 × 576 grid → 576 numbers out (each output = a weighted sum of all 576 inputs)

That one grid holds 576 × 576 = 331,776 numbers. Stack up all the grids in the model and you get 134.5 million numbers. These are the weights, also called parameters. Training means adjusting them, and they're what the model file stores. When later lessons delete a layer (08), round the weights to fewer bits (11) or add a small correction to a grid (13), these are the numbers being changed.

Key ideas

  • The tokenizer is a fixed rulebook (BPE), separate from the network. It turns text into IDs.
  • The embedding table turns each ID into a vector of 576 numbers by looking up a row.
  • Similar tokens have similar vectors before any layer has run.

Lesson 05 · lessons/05_anatomy.py

What's inside: one stream, thirty editors

Between the embedding table and the 49,152 scores, what is there?

Print the model in Python and you get the full parts list. It is surprisingly short: an embedding table, the same block repeated 30 times, a final normalisation and a scoring layer. Click the parts of the diagram to see what each one does.

The residual stream: the one idea to remember

Follow the thick amber line in the diagram. Each token has one vector of 576 numbers travelling up through the model. This is called the residual stream. Layers never replace it. Each one reads the stream, computes a small update, and adds the update back:

x = x + attention(norm(x)) x = x + mlp(norm(x))

A useful picture is a shared document with 30 editors working in turn. Each editor reads the current draft and appends a note, but nobody erases. Two kinds of editor alternate inside each block:

  • Attention is the only place where tokens exchange information. The vector for " of" can pull in information from the vector for "iffel". Lesson 07 is about this.
  • The MLP (multi-layer perceptron, also called the feed-forward layer) works on each token on its own. It's the biggest part of the model, and research suggests much of the model's stored knowledge lives here.

What's norm? Before each editor reads the stream, it takes a rescaled copy. This model uses RMSNorm: divide the vector by its typical size (the root of the mean of the squares), so its numbers sit around 1, then multiply each of the 576 dimensions by a learned weight. The stream keeps growing as updates pile up, and the norm means attention and the MLP always see numbers in a familiar range. It's one line:

norm(x) = x / sqrt(mean(x²) + ε) * w ε is a tiny number to avoid dividing by 0

What's inside the MLP? This model's MLP has a shape called SwiGLU. It makes two expanded copies of the token's vector, 576 → 1,536 numbers each. One copy goes through silu, a smooth "let positive values through, squash negatives toward 0", and is multiplied into the other, so it acts as a gate that decides how much of each of the 1,536 numbers passes. A third matrix shrinks the result back to 576. That's three matrices of 576 × 1,536 per layer, which is why the MLP holds most of the parameters.

mlp(h) = (silu(h · Wgate) * (h · Wup)) · Wdown

One consequence matters for the next lesson. Every layer reads 576 numbers per token and adds back 576 numbers, so the stream has the same shape after layer 15 as after layer 29. The final scoring step only needs "576 numbers for this token", so it can be run on the stream at any layer, not just the last. That's what makes lesson 06 possible.

Where the 134.5 million parameters live

MLPs hold 59%. The embedding table holds 21%, and it does double duty: the final scoring layer (lm_head) reuses the same matrix, a trick called tied embeddings. In an 8-billion-parameter model the embedding is only a few percent; small models spend a large share on vocabulary.

How much each layer writes into the stream

last token of our sentence, " of"

Our sentence

Look at the spike: the attention sublayers of layers 23 and 24 write updates of length about 190, about twice as big as the attention update in any other layer. (Length is the size of the 576-number update vector. The stream itself is about 300 long at that point, so one attention write moves it a lot.) Something important happens there for the token " of". Keep layers 23 and 24 in mind; they come back in the next three lessons.

Key ideas

  • A transformer is a lookup table, 30 identical "read, compute, add back" blocks, and a scoring layer.
  • The residual stream is the running vector per token. Layers add to it and never overwrite it.
  • Attention moves information between tokens. The MLP processes each token on its own.
  • Each sublayer reads a normalised copy of the stream (RMSNorm). The MLP expands, gates and shrinks (SwiGLU).

Lesson 06 · lessons/06_logit_lens.py

Watching it think: the logit lens

At which layer does the model "know" the answer is Paris?

The last step of the model turns the stream into scores: lm_head(norm(x)). It takes 576 numbers for a token and returns 49,152 scores. Every layer's output is also 576 numbers per token, the same shape (lesson 05). So nothing stops us from applying that last step in the middle of the network and asking: "if you had to answer right now, after layer 12, what would you say?"

It doesn't have to work. The scoring step was trained only on the stream after layer 29, and halfway up the numbers might mean something it can't read. The surprise is how readable they become well before the top.

That trick is called the logit lens. It's four lines of code and one of the most eye-opening things you can do with a model.

lessons/06_logit_lens.py

out = model(ids, output_hidden_states=True)     # the stream after every layer
for h in out.hidden_states:
    scores = model.lm_head(model.model.norm(h[0, -1]))  # the model's final step, applied early
    probs  = torch.softmax(scores, -1)                 # lesson 02

Read the stream after any layer

  • after layer 23: rank 142
  • after layer 24: rank 17
  • after layer 26: rank 2
  • after layer 27: rank 1, at 58%, rising to 88% at the output

Layers 23 and 24 again, the same place where attention made its big write in lesson 05. The early and middle layers aren't idle. They're building up context in a form this simple lens can't read yet. The late layers turn that context into a vocabulary answer.

Now pick the other prompts. "The capital of France is" is the prompt that failed in lesson 01. Its final answer is ' the', and ' Paris' ends at rank 2 with 9%. The model nearly knew. Rephrased as "The capital of France is the city of", ' Paris' wins with 79%. The fact is in the model; the phrasing decides whether it's the most plausible continuation.

The chart above only looks at the last position. Since the model predicts at every position (lesson 02), we can lens all of them at once. Each cell shows the top guess at that position after that layer; brighter means more confident.

Read each column bottom to top. Low layers often just echo the input token or produce filler. The column for "iffel" settles on ' Tower' early, a simple association. The column for " of" stays blank until the top few rows, where ' Paris' appears.

Key ideas

  • Because layers only add, the stream can be read at any depth with the model's own output step.
  • Answers form gradually and late. In this model the useful work for facts happens around layers 23–27.
  • "Wrong" answers often hide the right one at rank 2. Phrasing matters a lot for small models.

Lesson 07 · lessons/07_attention.py

Attention: how tokens talk to each other

The token " of" has to predict "Paris", but the clue is back at "iffel". How does it get there?

Attention is the only part of the model where one token can read another. Everything else (the MLP, the norms) works on each token separately. Here is the idea, without the matrix notation. Picture it from the point of view of " of", the last token of our sentence, which has to help predict the next word:

  • " of" makes a query: "what am I looking for?" Roughly, "which place is this sentence about?"
  • Every earlier token offers a key: "this is what I contain." "iffel" can offer something like "I'm part of a landmark's name"; "The" and "is" offer little.
  • Every earlier token also offers a value: "this is what I'll hand over if you pick me." For "iffel", that's information about the Eiffel Tower.

In one head, L23 H4, the query of " of" matches the key of "iffel" well, so " of" takes mostly "iffel"'s value. These descriptions are a way to picture it, not something written down inside the model: queries, keys and values are just 64-number lists, and the model learned during training what they come to mean. You'll see the real match below.

A token compares its query with every earlier token's key (a dot product, which is big when two vectors point the same way), turns those scores into weights with softmax (lesson 02 again), and takes a weighted average of the values. That's formula four:

weights = softmax(Q · Kᵀ / √64) who to look at (each row adds up to 1) output = weights · V what you collect from them

Note that "weights" here means attention weights: how much each token looks at each other token. These aren't the model's learned weights from lesson 04. They're computed fresh for every sentence.

Three details. A token may only look backwards (the causal mask); otherwise predicting the next token would be cheating. There are 9 heads in each of the 30 layers, 270 in total, each with its own queries and keys, so each head can learn its own kind of "looking". And each head works on its own slice of the stream: 576 numbers ÷ 9 heads = 64 numbers per query, key and value. That's the 64 in √64. One saving: the 9 heads don't each get their own keys and values. There are only 3 sets, each shared by 3 heads, which is called grouped-query attention. Lesson 10 shows why that matters. A dot product of 64 numbers can get large, and dividing by √64 = 8 keeps the scores in a range where softmax still spreads attention around instead of putting it all on one token.

The attention weights are just numbers we can pull out of the model. Pick a layer and head, then click any token to see where it looks.

Word order: how does attention know who came first?

Look at the formula again. It compares queries and keys, and nothing in it says where a token sits. On its own, attention would see "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" as the same bag of tokens. Yet some heads clearly care about position: the previous-token heads later in this lesson always look exactly one step back.

The fix in this model is RoPE (rotary position embedding). Just before the comparison, each query and key is rotated by an angle proportional to its position. The 64 numbers of each head are treated as 32 pairs, and each pair is rotated at its own speed, some fast like a seconds hand, some slow like an hour hand. Values are not rotated.

The trick: when you compare a rotated query with a rotated key, only the difference of their angles matters. So the score depends on how far apart two tokens are, not on where they sit in the document. Try it:

The attention sink side note · safe to skip

Select a random head and click a token. Very often most of the weight lands on the first token, "The". Averaged over all heads and layers, the first token gets 56% of all attention. It's not that "The" is important. Softmax forces the weights to add up to 1, so a head with nothing useful to do needs somewhere harmless to put its attention, and the model learned to park it on the first token. Researchers call this the attention sink.

Heads with jobs: the copying heads

Remember Robby from lesson 03? The second time the name came up, the model was certain of it, because it copied from earlier in the text. Now we can ask which heads do that copying.

To find them, we take meaning away so that copying is the only thing left that can work. Take 50 random tokens, gibberish, and repeat them twice. The model can't possibly predict the first copy, and no fact it has learned can help. But if it has a copying mechanism, the second copy is easy: "last time I saw this token, that token came next".

Loss on 50 random tokens, repeated twice

lower = better prediction

The first copy has an average loss of 12.75: pure guessing among 49,152 tokens. The second copy averages 0.66: the model predicts tokens it has seen only once, a few hundred milliseconds of compute earlier. This is in-context learning in its simplest form.

The heads responsible are called induction heads. On the second occurrence of token A, an induction head looks at whatever came after the first A. It works together with a simpler previous-token head in an earlier layer, which tags each token with "the token before me was …". Rows are the token doing the looking, columns the token looked at. A previous-token head draws a line just below the diagonal. An induction head draws a line in the lower-left block, offset by one: in the second copy, each token looks at the position just after its earlier twin. The sink head puts everything on column 0.

Copying thread

Back to the story: "…robot named Robby lived in a big city. Rob". At that second " Rob", head L18 H6, the strongest induction head, puts 97.5% of its attention on the token that followed the first " Rob": by. It carries "by" forward, and the model gives "by" 99.9%. The random-token experiment and the story show the same mechanism.

Key ideas

  • Attention = query·key scores → softmax → weighted average of values. Tokens only look backwards.
  • RoPE rotates queries and keys by position, so attention scores depend on how far apart tokens are.
  • 270 heads specialise: previous-token heads, induction (copy) heads, lookup heads, idle "sink" heads.
  • In our sentence, head L23 H4 carries "Eiffel" into the position that predicts "Paris".
  • Induction heads (layers 17–18) copy: at a repeated token they look at what followed it last time. That's how "Robby" came back.

Lesson 08 · lessons/08_break_it.py

Break it on purpose

Attention patterns are suggestive. How do we know a part actually matters?

Looking at attention weights is like watching where someone's eyes go: it tells you what they look at, not what they use. To find out what a part does, remove it and see what breaks. Neuroscientists call it a lesion study; in machine learning it's an ablation.

PyTorch makes this easy with hooks: functions you attach to any layer that run during the forward pass and can change what passes through. No retraining and no editing of weight files.

lessons/08_break_it.py

def skip_layer(i):                          # a layer computes x + update(x) ...
    def hook(mod, args, kwargs, output):
        return args[0]                           # ... so return x unchanged
    return layers[i].register_forward_hook(hook, with_kwargs=True)

def silence_head(layer, head):              # zero one head's 64 numbers before o_proj
    def pre_hook(mod, args):
        x = args[0].clone(); x[..., head*64:(head+1)*64] = 0
        return (x,)
    return layers[layer].self_attn.o_proj.register_forward_pre_hook(pre_hook)

Delete one layer at a time

30 versions of the model, each missing one layer

Our sentence

Switch the metric to p(' Paris'). With all layers in place the model gives ' Paris' 88%. Delete layer 23 and it falls to 3%. Layers 23, 0, 1 and 11 are the only ones whose loss destroys the answer. Layer 11 is a surprise, and we haven't explained it. It holds one of the strongest previous-token heads (lesson 07), which might be what lets the model treat ·E + iffel as one word, but that's a guess. Testing it with the lesson script makes a good exercise. Layer 23 is now confirmed four ways: the attention write (05), the lens (06), the lookup head (07), and this ablation.

Two other patterns stand out. Layer 0 is a disaster to remove for everything: it converts raw token lookups into a form the rest of the network expects. And most middle layers can disappear with only mild damage to ordinary text. Because each layer only adds to the stream, the others can partly cover for a missing one.

The copying metric tells a different story. Removing layer 16 (home of the strongest previous-token head) or layer 18 (strongest induction head) wrecks copying while ordinary text barely notices.

Silencing single heads

We can be more surgical and switch off one head at a time out of 270. Each cell below is one model with one head missing:

Interesting: the induction heads from lesson 07 barely show up one at a time. They back each other up. But switch off several together and copying collapses. To make that claim properly we need a control group: silence the same number of random heads and compare.

Top induction heads vs. random heads

loss on the second copy, lower = copying works

Silencing the top 5 induction heads (of 270) raises the loss from 0.66 to 10.5: copying is gone. Five random heads, averaged over 5 draws, give 0.87. Random heads from layers 0–1 are excluded on purpose: early heads break everything, and one of them can make a random group look "important for copying". The lesson script lets you remove that filter and watch the experiment go wrong.

Copying a made-up name

greedy continuation as induction heads are switched off

"Zorblat" is not a real name, so the model can't know it; the only way to produce it is to copy it from earlier in the prompt. With 3 heads off, the model reaches for "Quinta" (a piece of the bakery's name). With 10 off, it produces "Zaroff", a similar-sounding name: the memory of the name is blurring. With 30 off, it gives up and writes a common name.

Copying thread

This closes the thread. In lesson 03 the model repeated "Robby" with near certainty. In lesson 07 the induction heads in layers 17 and 18 were looking at exactly the right earlier token. Here, switching off the top 5 of them makes a repeat as hard to predict as gibberish (loss 0.66 → 10.5), while 5 random heads barely matter. "Zorblat" is the same test on real text: a name the model can only produce by copying, and it loses the name as the heads go.

Key ideas

  • Ablation is a causal test: remove a part, measure the damage, compare with a control.
  • Most single parts are backed up by others. Some (layer 0, layer 23 for our fact) are not.
  • This is the basic method of mechanistic interpretability, and it runs on a laptop.

Lesson 10 · lessons/10_kv_cache.py

    
The KV cache: why generation is fast

    
Our loop from lesson 03 re-runs the whole text for every token. Does it have to?

  

  

    
Look again at attention. When token 11 is generated, it needs the keys and values of tokens 1 to 10. But those never change: tokens only look backwards, so token 5's key can't depend on token 11. Recomputing them at every step is pure waste.

    
The fix: compute each token's keys and values once, store them, and at each new step feed in only the newest token. That store is the KV cache. It splits generation into two phases, which you'll see named in every inference server (llama.cpp, vLLM, MLX, Ollama):

    
    
      
  • Prefill: the whole prompt is processed in one parallel pass, filling the cache.
    • 
      
  • Decode: one token per step; read the whole cache, add one new row.
    • 
    

  

  

    
Prefill, then decode
each cell = one token's keys and values in one layer

    

      
      
      
    

    

    

  

  

    
Measured speed on an M4 Pro (MPS)
generating 50 tokens after prompts of different lengths

    

    
Without the cache, every step processes the whole prompt again, so speed collapses as the prompt grows: 64 tokens/s at 51 tokens of prompt, 11 tokens/s at 801. With the cache, speed barely depends on prompt length. The output is token-for-token identical either way.

  

  

    
The price: memory

    
The cache must hold keys and values for every layer, every KV head, and every token so far:

    
cache bytes = 2 (K and V) × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × tokens × bytes per number

    
The weights of a model have a fixed size. The cache grows with every token of context and with every user served in parallel. When you set a context size or a number of parallel slots in llama-server or Ollama, this is the number you're really choosing.

    
This is where grouped-query attention (GQA, lesson 07) pays off. The model has 9 query heads but only 3 key/value heads, each shared by 3 query heads. You can see it in the weight shapes: q_proj is 576 → 576, but k_proj and v_proj are 576 → 192 (3 × 64). The cache only stores keys and values, so it's 3 times smaller than with 9 full heads, at almost no cost in quality. That's the kv_heads in the formula.

  

  

    
KV cache calculator
pick a model, then change the context

    

    

      
      
      
    

    

  

  

    
What's slow now: reading the weights

    
With the cache in place, each decode step feeds in just one token. That's very little arithmetic, but the token still has to pass through every layer, so every weight is read from memory once per new token: about 270 MB for this model in 16-bit, gigabytes for bigger ones. Modern chips can do the arithmetic far faster than they can fetch the numbers, so the fetching sets the speed. Generation is memory-bound. Remember this; lesson 11 builds on it.

  

  

    
Key ideas

    
    
      
  • Past tokens' keys and values never change, so they're cached and reused.
    • 
      
  • Prefill processes the prompt in parallel; decode adds one token at a time.
    • 
      
  • The cache trades memory for speed and grows with context × users. GQA shrinks it.
    • 
      
  • During decode, every new token reads every weight once. Speed is limited by memory, not by arithmetic.
    •

Lesson 11 · lessons/11_quantization.py

    
Quantization: same model, fewer bits

    
The weights are 32-bit or 16-bit numbers. Do they need to be?

  

  

    
Lesson 10 ended on a fact: during decode, every new token means reading every weight from memory. The arithmetic is cheap; moving bytes is the slow part. So if each weight took fewer bytes, generation would be faster, and a bigger model would fit in the same RAM.

    
That's all quantization is: store each weight as a small integer (8, 4, sometimes 2 bits) plus a shared scale, and accept that the numbers are slightly off. The question is how far you can push it before the model stops saying Paris.

  

  

    
The memory bill
size of the weights alone = parameters × bytes per weight

    

    

    

  

  

    
Rounding 32 real weights

    
The simplest recipe is called absmax. Take a group of weights and find the biggest one. Make it the largest integer you have (127 for 8 bits, 7 for 4 bits). Divide every weight by that scale and round. You store the integers and the one scale. To use a weight, multiply it back.

    
scale = max(|w|) / 7
q     = round(w / scale)     ← stored as a small integer
w'    = q × scale            ← used at inference

    
Below are 32 real weights from SmolLM2. The top row shows the originals and the bottom row shows where they land after rounding. The faint lines are the only values the integers can represent. Try fewer bits, then switch to the group that contains an outlier.

  

  

    
Quantize a group of 32

    

      
      

    

    

    

    

  

  

    
The outlier is the enemy. One weight of −4.7 sets the scale for its whole group, and the normal weights, mostly around ±0.1 to ±0.5, get squashed onto zero and its neighbours. That's why real formats use small groups: each group of 32 gets its own scale, so an outlier only hurts 31 neighbours. In the lesson, the same 4-bit rounding over this whole matrix gives .

    
Small groups aren't free. Each one stores an extra 16-bit scale: 16 bits ÷ 32 weights = 0.5 bits per weight. So "4-bit, group 32" is really 4.5 bits per weight, and that's exactly the layout of llama.cpp's Q4_0 format.

    
Now the whole model

    
The lesson rounds every weight matrix to N bits with group size 32 and measures two things: perplexity on a paragraph (lesson 02's loss as exp(loss), lower is better) and agreement: how often the quantized model picks the same next token as the original.

Group size at 4 bits
perplexity, lower is better

      

      
One scale for the whole tensor destroys the model. Per-row scales are usable, and every halving of the group helps a bit more.

    

    

      
Which parts are fragile?
only that kind of matrix at 3 bits

      

      
Embeddings and the MLP's down_proj are the most sensitive; attention is the most forgiving. Real formats spend extra bits on the fragile parts.

    

  

  

    
Key ideas

    
    
      
  • Quantization = small integers + one scale per small group of weights.
    • 
      
  • 8 bits is almost free, 4 bits is the usual sweet spot, below that it collapses (sooner for small models).
    • 
      
  • Outliers are why groups exist, and why some tensors get more bits than others.

Lesson 12 · lessons/12_gguf.py

    
GGUF: one file that holds everything

    
llama.cpp, Ollama and LM Studio run a single .gguf file. What's inside it?

  

  

    
A Hugging Face model is a folder: weights, config.json, tokenizer.json and more. A GGUF is one file that carries all of it, laid out so a C++ program can map it straight into memory and start running.

    
The lesson downloads the official GGUF of the very same SmolLM2-135M (made by the llama.cpp team) and reads it with nothing but Python's struct module, about 30 lines. Click the parts of the file:

Two things to notice. The metadata is the config file plus the whole tokenizer: all 49,152 tokens from lesson 04 and 48,900 merge rules (the lesson checks they're identical). And the tensor names are shortened: model.layers.0.self_attn.q_proj.weight becomes blk.0.attn_q.weight.

    
Decoding a block by hand

Do the same comparison on attn_q and it fails badly, with a relative error over 100%. The numbers are right but the rows are in a different order. RoPE (lesson 07) rotates pairs of numbers. Hugging Face pairs element i with i + 32; llama.cpp pairs neighbours. Same math, so the converter shuffles the rows of q_proj and k_proj once. Undo the shuffle and the weights match. This only matters if you write your own converter or loader; nothing later depends on it.

What does "Q4_K_M" mean?

    
Quantized, 4-bit, K-quant, Medium mix. K-quants use blocks of 256 weights made of 8 sub-blocks of 32; each sub-block has its own scale and minimum, and those are themselves stored as 6-bit numbers. "Medium" means some fragile tensors get more bits. The lesson reads the real Q4_K_M file and finds a surprise:

These are the pure block formats. A file name like Q4_K_M or Q5_K_S is a mix: mostly the named format, with some tensors bumped up (S = small mix, M = medium, L = large). The file's actual bits per weight therefore sit a little above the format's number. Also count the tokenizer, which every GGUF carries.

  

  

    
Key ideas

    
    
      
  • A GGUF = metadata (config + tokenizer + chat template) + a tensor table + quantized blocks.
    • 
      
  • Q8_0, Q4_0, Q5_0 are lesson 11's recipe with groups of 32; K-quants use blocks of 256 with quantized scales.
    • 
      
  • A quant name is a recipe, not a promise: the file tells you what each tensor really got.

Lesson 13 · lessons/13_lora.py

    
LoRA: change a model by training 2% of it

    
Can we teach it that the Eiffel Tower is in Rome without touching its 135 million weights?

  

  

    
Fine-tuning normally updates every weight. For big models that's expensive: training needs several times the model's size in memory, and you end up with a full new copy of the model for every task.

    
LoRA (low-rank adaptation) freezes the original matrix W and learns a correction next to it, built from two thin matrices:

    
W_new = W + B × A

W: 576×576 (frozen)    B: 576×r    A: r×576

    
B × A has the same shape as W, but it is made from very few numbers. B starts at zero, so before training the model is exactly the original. Slide the rank:

  

  

    
W + B × A
one 576×576 attention matrix, drawn to scale

    

      
    

    

    

  

  

    
Training it

    
First, how training changes weights at all. Each step does three things:

    
    
      
  1. Run the model on the training text and measure the loss (lesson 02): how surprised it was by the right answer.
    2. 
      
  2. Work out, for every trainable number, whether nudging it up or down would lower the loss, and by how much. This is the gradient. PyTorch computes it automatically with loss.backward().
    3. 
      
  3. Nudge every trainable number a small step in the direction that lowers the loss, then repeat.
    4. 
    

    
The rule for how big each step is is called the optimizer. We use Adam, the standard choice, which adjusts each number's step size based on how its gradient has behaved recently.

    
This is the course's only training loop. It's lesson 02's loss on four sentences that place the tower in Rome, 60 steps, about 4 seconds on a laptop. Only A and B get updated, in every attention and MLP matrix of all 30 layers.

What does it believe now?

After the first run the tower is in Rome, even for a sentence it never saw. But so are the Statue of Liberty and the Sydney Opera House. Four examples taught it "famous landmark → Rome". The standard fix is anchors: true facts mixed into the training data that the adapter must leave alone. They stop the leak, but the edit can also generalise less: in this run the new wording fell back to Paris (on CPU it stayed Rome, since training differs slightly between devices). Getting a change to stick without spreading is the central difficulty of fine-tuning, with LoRA or without it.

    
The adapter is a dial

    
Because the original weights are untouched, you can scale the correction: W + s × B × A. At 0 you have the original model back, at 1 the full edit. One base model can serve many adapters and swap them per request.

Merge it, ship it, or train it on a 4-bit model

    
Merging: W·x + B·A·x = (W + B·A)·x, so you can add B × A into W once and get a normal model with zero extra cost per token. Shipping: or keep the adapter separate as a small file. llama.cpp loads one with --lora, Ollama with ADAPTER. QLoRA: the base is frozen anyway, so it can be stored in 4 bits (lesson 11) while the adapter trains in full precision. That's how people fine-tune 70B models on a single GPU.

  

  

    

  

  

    
Key ideas

    
    
      
  • LoRA freezes W and learns a thin B × A beside it: about 2% of the numbers, a few MB on disk.
    • 
      
  • It can be scaled, swapped, or merged into W for free inference.
    • 
      
  • It works on a quantized base (QLoRA). The hard part is the data, not the method.
